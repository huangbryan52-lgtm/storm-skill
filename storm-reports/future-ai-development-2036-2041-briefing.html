01

The 60-Second Summary

The one thing all five lenses agree on: by the late 2030s the binding constraint on AI will not be raw model intelligence — it will be diffusion. The cost of a fixed level of capability has already collapsed (roughly two orders of magnitude in two years) and will keep falling, which all but guarantees AI is embedded everywhere by 2036–2041 regardless of whether the capability curve keeps bending. But the payoff is gated by the unglamorous layers — compounding reliability over long task chains, integration into real workflows, and physical power/grid delivery — which is why ~95% of enterprise pilots show no return today. The genuinely contested question is capability: optimists read fast benchmark trends as a path to near-human general agents, skeptics point to scaling plateaus, a looming data wall, and reasoning models that break past a complexity threshold. The honest base case is a transformatively useful but jagged technology, delivering a real-but-modest productivity lift on a decade-long diffusion lag — not a 7%-of-GDP overnight leap, and not nothing. Expect a capital/valuation correction along the way; the overbuilt compute and energy infrastructure survives it and becomes the cheap substrate the 2040s winners inherit.

Five Key Findings, Ranked by Reliability

The cost of a fixed capability keeps collapsing — so AI is pervasive by the late 2030s whatever the capability ceiling
1
Reliability: High
9/10

Stanford HAI's 2025 AI Index measured the inference cost for a GPT-3.5-equivalent model falling roughly 280× (about $20 to $0.07 per million tokens) between November 2022 and October 2024. This is the single most robust trend in the whole picture: a measured, institutionally-sourced curve, not an extrapolation. It means deployment is essentially guaranteed — the economics of putting "good-enough" AI into every workflow only improve. Caveat held in check: cheaper tokens do not mean cheaper AI systems — total enterprise spend is rising as usage explodes, and inference is 80–90% of a production system's lifetime cost.

The binding constraint shifts from capability to diffusion: reliability, integration, and power
2
Reliability: Medium-High
8/10

Every lens converges here. MIT's 2025 study found ~95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots produced no measurable return — and blamed systems that "do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time," i.e. integration, not model IQ. Reliability: Medium

This is the sharpest quantitative disagreement in the panel. Bank/consultancy estimates are large: Goldman Sachs projects AI could lift global GDP ~7% (~$7T) over a decade and leave ~300M jobs exposed to automation (exposure, not forecast losses); McKinsey pegs $2.6–4.4T/yr of potential value. But MIT's Daron Acemoglu, modelling it from the task level, estimates no more than ~0.66% total-factor-productivity gain over a decade (an upper bound; his preferred estimate is lower) — roughly a tenth of the headline. The historical pattern favors the cautious end: general-purpose technologies deliver their productivity surge after a long reorganization lag, not on the hype timetable. Caveat: the bullish figures are 2023 bank/consultancy estimates from firms with an incentive to sell AI strategy work; the cautious figure is a transparent economic model (since peer-reviewed in Economic Policy, 2025) with its own contested assumptions.

"AI agents will handle month-long tasks by ~2030" (from the METR task-horizon extrapolation)

The METR doubling trend is real and striking, but the month-long-task figure is a log-linear extrapolation from ~6 years of data at 50% reliability on a software/ML benchmark — a preprint, not peer-reviewed, with unestablished construct validity and an 80%-reliability horizon that lags far behind. Projecting it ~15 years (≈26 doublings) is statistically indefensible. Track the curve as a leading indicator; do not state the date as a forecast.

03

The Hidden Connection

Finding 1 (collapsing cost) looks bullish and Finding 5 (capital correction) looks bearish — a contradiction. How can AI be "too cheap to meter" and a bubble at the same time?

Because they are the same fact. The very cost collapse that guarantees AI ends up everywhere is what destroys the margin of the model layer that is raising all the money. If intelligence is a cheap, swappable commodity, then value doesn't accrue to the model — it accrues to whoever owns the complementary bottleneck. Read across the lenses and the bottleneck has three names for one thing: the practitioner's "boring plumbing" (eval harnesses, retrieval, state, rollback), the economist's "compute-and-energy rentiers," and the historian's "overbuilt asset that survives the burst." The model is the part that commoditizes; the scaffolding around it, and the physical capacity underneath it, is where the durable rent sits.

The same curve that makes AI inevitable makes the AI model worthless as a moat — so the 2040s winners are the owners of the plumbing and the power, not the labs raising the money today.

The assumption this briefing rests on (and the missing 6th lens)

All five lenses treat AI's 10–15 year trajectory as a market-and-technology phenomenon — a story of cost curves, capability curves, capex, and diffusion that plays out through firms and investors. They freeze the variable that may matter most over this horizon: the geopolitical and governance regime. None addressed the US–China compute race, export controls, state and military deployment, sovereign-AI buildouts, or the regulatory response to a serious safety or misuse incident.

That is the omission that could invert the findings. If AI becomes a nationalized strategic asset — compute rationed by export regime, frontier models treated like weapons programs — the clean market logic of "cost collapses, diffusion wins, rentiers capture the rents" bends around state power and borders instead. A single catastrophic misuse event (or a crisis of public trust) could trigger diffusion-slowing regulation that makes the "slow payoff" base case look optimistic. Build the market scenario, then stress it against the one no lens priced in: politics.

04

What To Actually Do Differently

For a strategist, investor, policymaker, or builder placing bets whose payoff lands in the 2036–2041 window — concrete moves, not principles.

01
Underwrite the bottlenecks, not the models.

Durable value sits in the complementary scarce assets — power/grid interconnect, compute, cooling, and the integration "harness" (evals, retrieval, state, rollback). The model layer commoditizes as cost collapses (Findings 1 & the hidden connection). Own the picks and shovels, or the plumbing; rent the model.

02
Stress-test every bet against a capability plateau and a capex correction at once.

Assume the valuation reset happens (Finding 5). The decision test is: which of my assets survives the burst — i.e. is it the "dark fiber" someone inherits cheaply, or the pure-play that gets wiped out? Favor assets with value independent of the AGI timeline.

03
Treat ROI failure as an integration problem, not a model problem.

The ~95% pilot-failure rate (Finding 2) is about systems that don't retain feedback or fit the workflow. Scope narrowly, engineer reliability (verification/retry loops, human checkpoints), and buy down compounding error — a 99%-accurate model still fails ~18% of a 20-step task. Spend on the harness, not a bigger model.

04
Budget for a diffusion lag, not a step-change.

Model the payoff on a general-purpose-technology timetable (Finding 4, the dynamo pattern): a real but modest productivity lift arriving after years of organizational reorganization. Do not build a plan, a P&L, or a policy on the 7%-of-GDP headline landing this decade.

05
Track the task-horizon reliability curve as your leading indicator.

The single metric that would settle the capability debate is whether reliability compounds over long autonomous horizons. Watch the METR-style 80% (not 50%) horizon quarter over quarter; it tells you in advance whether the optimist or skeptic case is winning — before the market does.

06
Price in the missing 6th lens: geopolitics and regulation.

Keep an explicit hedge for export-control shocks, a US–China compute split, sovereign-AI mandates, and safety-incident-driven regulation (see the assumption box). The market scenario is the default; the political scenario is the one that can invert it.

What's Safe to Assert

✓ Safe — verified against primary source

  • Inference cost for a fixed capability level has fallen roughly two orders of magnitude (~280× for GPT-3.5-level, 2022–2024) and continues to drop — AI deployment is economically assured regardless of the capability ceiling.
  • The binding constraints are diffusion-side: integration/ROI, compounding reliability over long task chains, and data-center power, not raw model IQ.
  • Median AI researchers do not expect human-level machine intelligence by 2041 (Grace et al.: ~50% by 2047).
  • Data-center electricity demand is on track to roughly double by 2030 (IEA).

⚠ Say with a caveat

  • "~95% of enterprise GenAI pilots show no return" — attribute it to the MIT NANDA 2025 report; it's one widely-cited study, not a settled statistic.
  • The capex-vs-revenue "bubble" framing — the gap is real, but whether and when it corrects depends on revenue-ramp assumptions that are genuinely uncertain.
  • The economic payoff — cite the range (Acemoglu's ~0.66% TFP to Goldman's ~7% GDP) and name the dispute, don't pick one number.

✕ Don't assert

  • Any specific AGI/"human-level" date — the panel's own evidence says point forecasts are indefensible.
  • "AI agents will do month-long tasks by 2030" as fact — it's a fragile extrapolation from a preprint (see contested signal).
  • The 7%-of-GDP / $7T figure as established fact — it's an incentivized bank estimate at the top of a wide range.
  • That the bubble will "definitely" pop on any particular schedule — the direction is plausible, the timing is not knowable.
05

The Frontier Question

Does AI reliability compound over long, autonomous task horizons — or does it plateau?

This is the one empirical question that settles everything else. Benchmarks reward single-shot answers; the economy pays for unsupervised execution of 100-step tasks inside messy institutions. If per-step reliability keeps climbing and long-horizon success rates follow, the optimist case — transformative, economy-reshaping agents — comes into view this decade. If reliability plateaus (as the compounding-error math and reports of reasoning-model breakdown past a complexity threshold suggest it might), AI stays a powerful-but-supervised tool and the modest-payoff base case holds. No lens could resolve it because the data to answer it — long, unsupervised, real-world agentic track records — does not yet exist. Whoever watches that one curve most honestly will see the 2036–2041 world arriving before everyone else.
